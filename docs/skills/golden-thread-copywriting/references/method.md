# Working method

This is an original synthesis of the supplied worksheets and annotated examples. Framework names retain their source terminology. The operating decisions below adapt them to current, evidence-based work; they are not missing course-video transcripts.

## 1. Research what changes the message

Collect audience language from current interviews, questions, reviews, sales conversations, support records, and approved first-party research. Study the existing destination and buying sequence. Product documents, an actual demonstration, and people who use or sell the product often reveal better specifics than a generic persona.

Capture only useful, supported details:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Present situation | What is happening when they encounter the message? |
| Desired change | What do they want to have, do, or feel differently? |
| Unspoken question | What uncertainty might prevent the next action? Label a hypothesis as such. |
| Constraints | Time, travel, effort, price, trust, suitability, or other stated obstacles |
| Existing knowledge | Which explanation can we build on? Which term needs defining? |
| Own language | Approved exact words or careful paraphrases with source context |
| Evidence | Facts, examples, demonstrations, records, and limitations |

Do not fill demographic fields merely because a worksheet contains them. Income, politics, personal beliefs, or vulnerabilities should not be fabricated or collected unnecessarily. A persona is a writing aid, not a verified profile of every reader.

## 2. Describe the change

The Before/After Grid asks about Have, Feel, Average Day, Status, and Good vs Evil. Use the relevant rows and leave the rest out. Describe the before situation without insulting the reader; describe a plausible after possibility without promising an entire life transformation.

Example for a calendar lead magnet:

- Before: “I'd like more time outside, but haven't pictured where it fits.” This is a writing hypothesis until audience research confirms it.
- After: “I can see some possible visits, with the people, places, and rhythm I chose.” This follows the actual function.
- Evidence: editable calendar generated from stated choices.
- Next action: create the calendar, then explore a trial stay.

The deeper emotional benefit might be anticipation or familiarity. Frame it as an invitation, not a guaranteed psychological outcome.

## 3. Connect features to meaning

Use a chain: **feature → what it enables → why the reader cares → evidence or limit**.

For example, recurring access enables repeat visits. Repeat visits create opportunities to get to know a landscape over time. The reader may value having somewhere to return to. The membership terms support access, but do not guarantee dates, wildlife sightings, belonging, or happiness.

If “So what?” produces an unsupported leap, revise to a smaller, defensible benefit. Concrete product detail can itself paint a benefit, as in the food, books, and car samples. Do not erase all features or hide material mechanics in pursuit of emotion.

## 4. Write the positioning sentence

Internal template: “For [reader], [product] helps [meaningful change] through [specific mechanism], supported by [evidence].”

The USP worksheet uses an “only one” formula. Use that claim publicly only if current research supports it. Otherwise state what the product does distinctly and concretely. A vague superlative is not a USP.

## 5. Choose an opening that meets awareness

The supplied six-lead handout distinguishes direct and indirect approaches. Existing product familiarity is a cue, not a rigid rule. A new product can still have a clear, direct promise.

| Lead | Appropriate use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Offer | Reader knows the product and needs the actual offer | Inventing a discount, free trial, or deadline |
| Promise | A clear, credible benefit can lead | Guaranteed transformation unsupported by the product |
| Problem-solution | Reader recognises a concrete problem; mechanism answers it | Exaggerating distress or manufacturing a problem |
| Secret/system | A real unfamiliar method deserves explanation | Calling ordinary information a secret; hiding material facts |
| Declaration | A verified relevant development changes the decision | Fabricated news, predictions presented as certainty |
| Story | A relevant documented experience or clearly illustrative scene can build understanding | Fake customers, invented quotes, disguised fiction |

Choose a small number of genuinely different openings when exploring variants. Keep the body and destination aligned with the selected lead. Resolve curiosity with useful information rather than endlessly postponing the answer.

## 6. Plan the thread before polishing lines

Use a compact map:

| Section/touch | Reader question | New meaning or evidence | Natural next question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Why is this relevant to me? | One meaningful promise | Is this a real possibility? |
| Explanation | How could it work? | Mechanism and material limits | What would it look like for me? |
| Picture | Can I recognise my life here? | Concrete scene or demonstration | Why should I believe it? |
| Proof/clarity | Can I trust the claim and understand the terms? | Relevant evidence and answers | What can I do next? |
| Action | What happens if I click? | Specific next-step benefit and destination | Delivered by the destination |

The order can change. Proof may belong near the opening for a skeptical audience. Short WhatsApp copy may do all five jobs in three sentences. The map is an editing tool, not a compulsory visible layout.

## 7. Check completeness without forcing formulas

- **4Ps:** Promise, Picture, Proof, Push. A useful invitation can complete the sequence without pressure.
- **4Us:** Unique, Useful, Urgent, Ultra-specific. Specificity must be real. Genuine urgency is optional, not a license to add a countdown.
- **Four-legged stool:** Track record, Credibility, Big idea, Future benefit. Note missing evidence; do not manufacture a track record to fill a leg.
- **Sales-letter anatomy:** headline, lead, argument, close, optional extra benefit, guarantee, PS. These are available devices, not required sections for every medium.

## 8. Learn from results correctly

The example books report selected historical successes, often with incomplete measurement or estimates. They show possible structures, not universal causal proof. “Control” means the publisher says that promotion was their comparator at that time.

In a live experiment, name the audience, traffic source, change, and desired downstream action. Separate opens, clicks, enquiries, qualified requests, payments, refunds, and retention. An attention-grabbing headline can win clicks while attracting unsuitable leads. Measure the business action and the journey, not just attention.
